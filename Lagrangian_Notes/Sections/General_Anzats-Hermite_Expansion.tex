\section{Gaussian Ansatz: Hermine Expansion}

\subsection{Hermite Polynomials}

There are two different ways of standardizing the Hermite polynomials:

\begin{itemize}
\item The probabilists Hermite polynomials are given by 
\begin{equation}
\mathit{He}_n(x)=(-1)^n e^{\frac{x^2}{2}}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}=\left (x-\frac{d}{dx} \right )^n \cdot 1,
\end{equation}
\item The physicists Hermite polynomials are given by
\begin{equation}
H_n(x)=(-1)^n e^{x^2}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}=\left (2x-\frac{d}{dx} \right )^n \cdot 1. 
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
These two definitions are not exactly identical; each one is a rescaling of the other,

\begin{equation}
H_n(x)=2^{\tfrac{n}{2}}{\mathit{He}}_n(\sqrt{2} \,x), \qquad \mathit{He}_n(x)=2^{-\tfrac{n}{2}}H_n\left(\frac x{\sqrt{2}} \right).
\end{equation}

These are Hermite polynomial sequences of different variances; see the material on variances below.

The notation $\mathit{He}$ and $\mathit{H}$ is that used in the standard references Abramowitz and Stegun. 
The polynomials $\mathit{He}$  are sometimes denoted by $\mathit{H}$, especially in probability theory, because
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}
\end{equation}
is the probability density function for the normal distribution with expected value 0 and standard deviation 1.
The first eleven probabilists' Hermite polynomials are:
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_0(x)=1\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_1(x)=x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_2(x)=x^2-1\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_3(x)=x^3-3x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_4(x)=x^4-6x^2+3\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_5(x)=x^5-10x^3+15x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_6(x)=x^6-15x^4+45x^2-15\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_7(x)=x^7-21x^5+105x^3-105x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_8(x)=x^8-28x^6+210x^4-420x^2+105\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_9(x)=x^9-36x^7+378x^5-1260x^3+945x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}{\mathit{He}}_{10}(x)=x^{10}-45x^8+630x^6-3150x^4+4725x^2-945\,\end{equation}
and the first eleven physicists' Hermite polynomials are:
\begin{equation}H_0(x)=1\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_1(x)=2x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_2(x)=4x^2-2\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_3(x)=8x^3-12x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_4(x)=16x^4-48x^2+12\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_5(x)=32x^5-160x^3+120x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_6(x)=64x^6-480x^4+720x^2-120\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_7(x)=128x^7-1344x^5+3360x^3-1680x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_8(x)=256x^8-3584x^6+13440x^4-13440x^2+1680\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_9(x)=512x^9-9216x^7+48384x^5-80640x^3+30240x\,\end{equation}
\begin{equation}H_{10}(x)=1024x^{10}-23040x^8+161280x^6-403200x^4+302400x^2-30240\,\end{equation}

As an alternative to calculating $N^{th}$ order derivatives of $e^{-x^2/2}$  and $e^{-x^2}$, 
an easier, less computationally intensive method of sequentially deriving individual terms of 
the $N^{th}$ order Hermite polynomials is to consider the combination of coefficients in the 
corresponding terms in the $N?1^{th}$ Hermite polynomial. For the probabilists' notation, follow 
the following rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item For the starting point in the sequence, the 0th-order polynomial $H_0$ is equal to 1.
\item The first term has a power of $x$ equal to the given $N^{th}$-order polynomial being derived, 
and the coefficient of this term is 1.
\item The power of $x$ of each successive term is 2 less than the preceding term.
\item The coefficient of each term after the first term is calculated by taking the coefficient 
of the same-numbered term in the $N^{th}$ polynomial, and adding to it the product 
of the power of $x$ and the corresponding coefficient of the immediately preceding term in the N^{th} polynomial.
\item All even-numbered terms in each polynomial are negative, and all odd-numbered terms are positive.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Properties}
H_n is a polynomial of degree n. The probabilists' version He has leading coefficient 1, 
while the physicists' version H has leading coefficient 2^n.

\subsection{Orthogonality}
$H_n(x)$ and $He_n(x)$ are $n^{th}$-degree polynomials for $n= 0, 1, 2, 3,\dots$.  
These orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight function
\begin{equation}
(He): w(x) = e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}
\end{equation} 
or
\begin{equation}
(H): w(x) = e^{-x^2}
\end{equation}
\ie, we have
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty H_m(x) H_n(x)\, w(x) \, \mathrm{d}x = 0, \qquad m \neq n.
\end{equation}

Furthermore,
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty {\mathit{He}}_m(x) \mathit{He}_n(x)\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} \, \mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{2 \pi} n! \delta_{nm}    (probabilistic)
\end{equation}
or
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty H_m(x) H_n(x) e^{-x^2}\, \mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{ \pi} 2^n n! \delta_{nm} (physicists')
\end{equation}.
The probabilist polynomials are thus orthogonal with respect to the standard normal probability density function.

\subsection{ Completeness }
The Hermite polynomials (probabilist or physicist) form an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space of functions satisfying
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2\, w(x) \, \mathrm{d}x <\infty,
\end{equation}
in which the inner product is given by the integral including the Gaussian weight 
function $w(x)$ defined in the preceding section,
\begin{equation}
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\overline{g(x)}\, w(x) \, \mathrm{d}x.
\end{equation}

An orthogonal basis for $L^2(\mathrm{R},w(x)dx)$ is a complete orthogonal system.  
For an orthogonal system, completeness is equivalent to the fact that the 0 function is the only function 
$f \in L^{2}(R,w(x) dx) orthogonal to all functions in the system.

Since the linear span of Hermite polynomials is the space of all polynomials, 
one has to show (in physicist case) that if $f$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) x^n e^{- x^2} \, \mathrm{d}x = 0
\end{equation}
for every $n \geq 0$, then $f =0$.

One possible way to do this is to appreciate that the entire function
\begin{equation}
F(z) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{z x - x^2} \, \mathrm{d}x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{n!}\int f(x) x^n e^{- x^2} \, \mathrm{d}x = 0
\end{equation}
vanishes identically. The fact then that $F(it)=0$ for every $t$ real 
means that the Fourier transform of $f(x)exp(?x^2)$ is 0, hence $f$ is 
0 almost everywhere. Variants of the above completeness proof apply to 
other weights with exponential decay.

In the Hermite case, it is also possible to prove an explicit identity 
that implies completeness (see section on the Completeness relation below).

An equivalent formulation of the fact that Hermite polynomials are an orthogonal 
basis for $L^{2}(R,w(x)dx)$ consists in introducing Hermite functions (see below), 
and in saying that the Hermite functions are an orthonormal basis for $L^2(R)$.




We begin by using a Gaussian ansatz:
\begin{equation}
    u(\vect{r}_{\perp},\vect{p}(z)) = I(\vect{p}) \exp{- \left(\Theta(\vect{r}_{\perp},\vect{p}) + i \Phi(\vect{r}_{\perp},\vect{p}) \right)}
\end{equation}
Now, lets calculate each term in the Lagrangian density:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}

   -2 n_0 \alpha \Im{\partial_{z}(u)\conj{u}} &= 2 n_0 \alpha \left(\nabla_{\vect{p}}{\Phi}\cdot\vect{\dot{p}}\right) |u|^2 \\    
   &= 2 n_0 \alpha \left(\nabla_{\vect{p}}{\Phi}\cdot\vect{\dot{p}}\right) I^2(\vect{p})\exp{-2 \Theta(\vect{r}_{\perp},\vect{p})} \\    
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

\end{split}

\subsection{Euler-Lagrange equation(s)}
Now, we can apply the Euler-Lagrange equations to the reduced density $\mathcal{F}_{D}(\vect{p},\dot{\vect{p}})$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \der{\mathcal{F}_{D}}{p_i}  - \der{}{z}\left(\pder{\mathcal{F}_{D}}{\dot{p_i}}\right) = 0 
\end{split}
\end{equation}
for $p_i = A, W_x,W_y; T_x, T_y, X, Y; F_x,F_y, P$.
So lets calculate the terms on the left side of each:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \der{F_{D}}{A}  &=  2 n_0 \alpha\, (2A) \left(\dot{P}  + \frac{\dot{F}_{x} }{2W_{x}^2} + \frac{\dot{F}_{y}}{2W_{y}^2} \right) - 2A  \left(\dot{X}T_x + \dot{Y} T_y\right)  - A  \left(W_x^2+ W_y^2\right) \\

\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
